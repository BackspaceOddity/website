What this document is\nNobody needs another film or series. What people need are specific things that films and series can sometimes give them: a rush of fear in a safe room, a story to share with someone they love, an hour of escape after a hard day, a world that makes them feel less alone. These needs are real — but they're not exclusive to cinema. A person sitting on the couch on a Friday night can do dozens of things: scroll through Instagram, rewatch The Office for the fifth time, call a friend, go for a run, start a video game. The question is never \"will they watch something?\" — it's \"in this moment, with these people, in this mood, will they choose us?\"\nThat framing changes everything. Competition is not just other horror films. It is immersive theater, true crime podcasts, narrative video games — other formats serving the same emotional need. It is also social media and comfort rewatches — options that don't even try to tell a new story, but win because they're frictionless and familiar. To earn someone's attention, a Story Direction doesn't just have to be better than other films. It has to be worth choosing over everything else that moment could become.\nAt the founding strategic session, Puma in a Tank's team articulated a clear ambition: to build not just a production company, but a company that thinks like a product company. Not in the sense of building software — but in the sense of a mindset: how we prioritize decisions, how we understand audience needs, how we define what success looks like before we spend money finding out. In product companies, the starting point is always the same — who is the audience, what do they need, and what would make our solution so much better that they'd switch to it and stay? The same logic applies here. Any piece of content PiaT produces is a product built for a specific audience in specific situations, competing for their attention against everything else they could do.\nA Story Direction is not a creative exercise for its own sake. It is an answer — a set of risky hypotheses about how a specific combination of world, character, conflict, and promise can close a specific audience gap in a specific context. This document describes the system for testing whether that answer is right, and which hypothesis to test next on the way to finding out.\nThe Cascade Navigation System is a framework for managing this work in an open-sea environment — where the team doesn't have roads or marked paths, only a compass (audience need) and a way to take readings. Each reading doesn't just confirm the current heading. It redraws the entire chart. This is navigation, not validation. The goal is to find the best next heading that opens up the broadest stretch of sea ahead — starting from the audience need in a specific moment, not from the script.\n---\n## 2. The Methodology: Three Pillars\nThe system stands on three integrated pillars. Understanding how they work together is essential before diving into the details.\n### Pillar 1: Cascading Hypotheses (The Core)\nCascading hypotheses is the central mechanism of the entire system. It is how a single experiment updates the entire map.\n**What cascading means:** When the team tests one hypothesis, the result doesn't just confirm or deny that single claim. It cascades outward, changing confidence in every connected hypothesis — including hypotheses in different Blocks and different stages. One reading, many updates.\n#### **How it works**\nHypotheses are organized in strict sequence: Type 1 (Block 1 — Audience) inform Type 2 (Block 2 — Product), which inform Type 3 (Block 3 — Growth Engine).\n- Type 1 hypotheses answer: Does a real audience with a real gap exist in a specific competitive context? (JTBD, Category Entry Points, Target Audience, Problems, Competitive Landscape; detailed in Section 3)\n- Type 2 hypotheses answer: Does this specific Story Direction close that gap better than alternatives? (Hook, Components, Series sustain; detailed in Section 3)\n- Type 3 hypotheses answer: Can we build a repeatable machine to reach this audience at scale? (CAC, Retention, Flywheel, Adjacent expansion; detailed in Section 3)\nWhen an experiment confirms a Type 1 hypothesis — for example, \"solo horror fans in their late 20s have an unmet need for immersive short-form content on Friday nights\" — that confirmation cascades forward: it raises confidence in all Type 2 hypotheses testing whether this story works for this audience. If the JTBD is real and the gap is real, the story has a better chance of being the right answer.\nConversely, if an experiment denies a Type 1 hypothesis — if the audience doesn't exist as described, or the competitive gap is smaller than expected — that denial cascades backward to reshape every downstream hypothesis. All the Type 2 and Type 3 hypotheses built on that audience understanding become suspect. Priorities change. The team may need to pivot to a different audience or entry point entirely, not because the story is weak, but because its foundation shifted.\n#### Experiments change probabilities, which change priorities\nEach week, after an experiment runs, the team re-rates affected hypotheses. Confidence scores change based on new evidence. When Confidence changes, the Prospect Score (Confidence × Opportunity) changes. When Prospect Score changes, the ranking of what to test next changes. The team doesn't test the same hypothesis twice unless circumstances force it. The entire priority list redraws with each reading.\n#### *P(success)* as strategic compass\n*P(success)* is the team's collective estimate of the probability that a Story Direction reaches commercial viability. It is not a formula. It is a judgment, informed by the hypothesis scores but not mechanically derived from them. After each cascade update, the committee asks: \"Given everything we know now — what is our overall confidence that this direction makes it?\" Each member gives a number (0–100%). The average is *P(success)*.\nRising *P(success)* after an experiment = the team is gaining sea room. The direction is opening up. Flat *P(success)* after three experiments = something structural is wrong — the fundamentals aren't shifting, which means the experiments aren't changing the picture. Falling *P(success)* = the direction is weakening; this may be a signal to pivot or kill.\n#### Why this matters:\nTraditional validation asks: \"Is this idea good?\" Cascading hypotheses asks: \"Given what we know now, which hypothesis — if confirmed — would open up the broadest stretch of sea ahead?\" This shifts the team from proving things are good to choosing the best next heading. And because each heading is a testable claim with a falsification condition, the team knows exactly what would change their mind.\n---\n### Pillar 2: Category Entry Points (The Optics)\nA Category Entry Point (CEP) is a situation or context in which a person experiences a need that leads them to seek content in our category. It is not a demographic. It is not a psychographic profile. It is a moment.\n#### What CEPs are:\nExamples for horror/thriller content:\n- \"Friday night alone, want adrenaline\" — lean-back, long-form, immersive\n- \"Commute, 15 minutes, need distraction\" — short-form, mobile, high-hook\n- \"With friends, want shared fear experience\" — social, shareable, reaction-worthy\n- \"Can't sleep, need tension to focus the mind\" — audio or slow-burn, intimate\nEach entry point implies different content format, length, platform, and tone. The same JTBD (\"I want to be captivated by something thrilling\") activates differently in different contexts.\n#### CEP as a lens, not a context:\nCEPs are the optics through which we look at everything. The same story direction can be weak in one CEP and dominant in another. Different CEPs have different competitors, different positioning, different audience needs. When the team maps a CEP and identifies current alternatives, they are mapping the competitive landscape in that specific moment. Friday night alone has Netflix horror and comfort rewatches as competitors. A commute with 15 minutes has podcasts and TikTok. These are different battlegrounds.\nCEP is the lens that focuses the hypothesis beam. Without CEP focus, hypotheses are too abstract to test. With CEP focus, every hypothesis is specific and testable.\n#### How hypotheses look through the CEP lens:\nInstead of: \"People want thrilling content\"\nThrough CEP lens: \"People home alone on Friday nights, looking for an adrenaline rush, will prefer our 20-minute found-footage hook over Netflix horror because we're shorter and more immersive\"\nInstead of: \"Our hook is strong\"\nThrough CEP lens: \"Our hook captures attention of people in the primary CEP (solo evening viewing) over their current alternatives, as measured by engagement rate 4%+ with comment evidence of genre recognition\"\nCEP is the filter. It makes every hypothesis testable.\n#### CEPs and flexibility during cascade:\nStage 1 designates a primary entry point to focus product validation (Stages 2–4). But CEP is not fixed. If Stages 2–3 experiments show the product works in a different entry point, or fails in the primary CEP, the team re-evaluates. CEP approval happens by end of Stage 3 (Signal Phase). Stages 4–5 (Commitment Phase) operate with a committed CEP — no changes within the phase. But between Signal and Commitment, CEP can pivot. The team may discover the best market is adjacent to where they started.\n#### CEPs relate to JTBD:\nJTBD answers: What does a person need?\nCEP answers: When and where does that need become active?\nTogether: \"Person in situation X experiences need Y, and current options don't satisfy them because Z.\" This is specific enough to design an experiment around.\n#### Attribution:\nThe CEP concept originates from Byron Sharp and Jenni Romaniuk at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for Marketing Science. Published in \"How Brands Grow\" (2010) and \"How Brands Grow Part 2\" (2016), it was developed for FMCG and consumer brands — around the idea of \"mental availability\" and the cues that trigger consumers to think about a category in specific moments. Applying this framework to entertainment and content is novel. The traditional content industry thinks in demographics and genres. CEPs reframe the question: In what moment of need does our content win?\n---\n### Pillar 3: Stage-Gate (The Operational Realization)\nStage-Gate is how cascading hypotheses are operationalized. Stages exist to test specific groups of hypotheses at the lowest cost that produces credible answers. Gates are the decision checkpoints where the team reviews Confidence movement and decides: Go, Pivot, or Kill.\n#### Stages as hypothesis tests:\n- **Prep:** Initial plausibility check. Does this direction warrant deeper research?\n- **Stage 1:** Tests Block 1 hypotheses (Is the audience real? Is there a gap? Can we reach them?). Ends with a Target Audience Brief and CEP Map.\n- **Stage 2:** Tests Block 2, first signal. Does the story resonate with early adopters in the primary CEP?\n- **Stage 3:** Tests Block 2, repeatable proof. Do the components work independently?\n- **Stage 4:** Tests Block 2, integrated proof, and early Block 3 signals. Does the series sustain? Are we seeing growth signals?\n- **Stage 5:** Tests Block 3 at scale. Can we build a repeatable growth engine?\n#### Two phases:\nThe system runs in two distinct phases, which change the decision tool but not the intensity of work.\n**Signal Phase (Stages 1–3):** The team is choosing between competing possibilities. Which audience to investigate first? Which CEP to commit to? Which hook framing? The decision tool is the Prospect Score ranking — at each weekly meeting, the team asks \"which hypothesis, if confirmed, opens up the most?\" and tests from the top of the list. Low Confidence, high Opportunity = often the right test because it's uncertain.\n**Commitment Phase (Stages 4–5):** The team has committed to one direction. The decision tool shifts to failure-mode analysis: \"what can still go wrong?\" The team is no longer ranking multiple headings. It is sailing the chosen course and asking what remains to be proven about that course.\nThe IC Chair calls this transition explicitly at the end of Stage 3: \"We are no longer choosing between headings — we are sailing this course.\"\n#### Gate decisions:\nAt each gate, the Navigation Lead (or Growth Lead at Gate 5) writes a Gate Memo: \"We entered this stage with hypothesis H scoring \\[Prospect Score\\]. After experiments, Confidence moved from \\[X\\] to \\[Y\\]. *P(success)* moved from \\[A%\\] to \\[B%\\]. Recommendation: Go / Pivot / Kill.\"\n<table header-row=\"true\">\n<tr>\n<td>Confidence at gate end</td>\n<td>Gate recommendation</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>7+ on primary hypothesis</td>\n<td>Go — evidence is strong; move to the next stage</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>5–6 on primary hypothesis</td>\n<td>Hold / Pivot — signal exists but isn't conclusive; refine and retest</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Below 5 after multiple experiments</td>\n<td>Kill — the hypothesis isn't holding; don't spend more</td>\n</tr>\n</table>\n### How the Three Pillars Converge\n**Cascading hypotheses** provide the thinking mechanism — how confidence updates propagate through the system.\n**Category Entry Points** provide the specificity — the lens that makes every hypothesis testable and every experiment grounded in a real moment of audience choice.\n**Stage-Gate** provides the operational container — how and when tests happen, at what cost.\nTogether, they create a system where:\n- Every hypothesis is testable because it is anchored to a CEP (Pillar 2)\n- Every test cascades outward because hypotheses are organized in informed sequences (Pillar 1)\n- Every test happens at the right time and cost level because stages are designed to test specific hypothesis clusters (Pillar 3)\nA team operating this system asks: \"Which hypothesis, if confirmed in which CEP, would open up the broadest route to success?\" and \"What is the cheapest experiment that could meaningfully move Confidence?\" These are the two questions the team cycles through, week after week.\n---\n## 3. Three Blocks\nThe entire system is built around three Blocks. Every hypothesis the team tests, every experiment it runs, every gate decision it makes belongs to one of them. The Blocks are answered in strict sequence: Block 1 → Block 2 → Block 3. Each Block is a cluster of sub-questions answered through coordinated experiments within the Category Entry Point optics. Each confirmed Block raises confidence in the next.\n### Block 1: Audience — \"Is there an underserved audience we can win?\"\n#### Sub-questions:\n- **JTBD (Job to be Done):** Is this audience need real and genuinely underserved? The root hypothesis — the foundation the entire cascade stands on. The JTBD is not a genre preference. It is a functional or emotional need (\"I want to distract myself with something captivating\").\n- **Category Entry Points:** In what situations does this JTBD activate? The team maps entry points where the need becomes acute enough for a person to seek content. For each: situation description, frequency, current alternatives, gap assessment. The team can map as many entry points as discovered, but prioritize the top 3 they believe in most strongly. One is designated primary CEP for Stages 2–4.\n- **Target Audience:** Who specifically feels this need most acutely in the primary CEP? Describe them in a way that allows the team to find them and engage them in experiments. The key test: \"Can we find 50–100 of these people for our first experiment?\" Not \"millennials who like horror\" — a specific person in a specific moment that the team can locate and recruit.\n- **Audience Problems:** What specifically does current content fail to provide for this Target Audience in the primary CEP? Not \"they want better horror.\" Rather: \"when they're alone Friday night looking for adrenaline, available options are either too shallow or too demanding — nothing fills the gap for a 20-minute immersive experience.\"\n- **Competitive Landscape:** Who currently tries to serve this need in the primary CEP, and where do they fall short? CEP-specific: different entry points have different competitors. The team maps direct (same job, same format), secondary (same job, different format), and indirect (different job, competing for same moment).\nAll five sub-questions must hold for Block 1 to be answered. If the JTBD is real but no specific entry points can be identified — the need is too diffuse. If entry points exist but no Target Audience can be found — the need is too broad. If Target Audience exists but the competitive landscape is closed — there is no gap to close.\n#### On abstraction levels:\nThe framework works at any level of abstraction. From very broad (\"families want good weekend movies that are not dumb comedies but teach something\") to very specific (\"solo horror fans want 20-min immersive content on Friday nights\"). The team chooses the zoom level. This is the beauty and universality of the framework — it's not forcing niche thinking. If the market opportunity is broad, the hypotheses can stay broad. If the team wants precision, it can zoom in. The cascade logic works at every level.\nExamples at different abstraction levels:\n- **Broad:** \"Young people (18–35) want to feel genuinely scared in a way that's shareable with friends.\"\n- **Specific:** \"Solo horror fans in their late 20s want 20-minute immersive experiences on Friday nights when they're home alone and looking for adrenaline.\"\nBoth are valid starting points. The team's research, then experiments, will calibrate whether broad or specific is the better entry.\n#### CEP optics at Block 1 output:\nThe CEP lens is already applied when the team formulates Block 1 hypotheses and prepares Gate 1 artifacts. CEP thinking begins in Stage 1 research and is embedded in every hypothesis from Block 1 onward. It's not something that starts in Block 2. When the team emerges from Stage 1, their Target Audience, Problems, and Competitive Landscape should all be described through the lens of specific entry points.\n#### Block 1 conclusion:\nBy end of Stage 1, the team has formulated and selected the strongest hypotheses about audience, need, and entry points. This is not confirmation — confirmation comes from Stages 2–4 testing. If Block 2 experiments don't confirm audience hypotheses, the team returns to Block 1 to reformulate. This IS the cascade logic. The system is built for iteration, not linear march.\n---\n### Block 2: Product — \"Does our content win against alternatives?\"\nOnce the team has identified a real audience with a real gap in a specific entry point, Block 2 asks whether *this specific Story Direction* can close that gap better than existing alternatives within the primary CEP.\n#### Sub-questions:\n- **Does the hook beat competitors in this CEP?** Does a single artifact stop a scroll, make the audience promise resonate, and frame the genre correctly for a person in the primary entry point? (Stage 2)\n- **Do the components generate independent investment?** Isolated tests of Conflict, Character, and World — one variable at a time, each within the same primary CEP — prove which creative elements drive the direction's strength independently. (Stage 3)\n- **Does the series sustain across episodes?** Does the concept hold across 4–6 episodes within the primary CEP? Do people come back? Does community form? (Stage 4)\n- **Can we reach early adopters effectively?** Channel strategy and early acquisition: which platform and format gives best access to the Target Audience in the primary CEP, and at what cost? (Stages 2–4)\nBlock 2 is answered through Stages 2–4. It is the most resource-intensive phase because the team is now producing live content and running tests in market. When answering Block 2, all sub-questions are hypotheses focused through the primary CEP lens.\n---\n### Block 3: Growth Engine — \"Can we build a scalable and repeatable audience growth engine?\"\nOnce Block 2 proves the story works for early adopters in the primary CEP, Block 3 asks whether the team can build a scalable, predictable, and sustainable acquisition machine.\n#### Sub-questions:\n- **Scalable acquisition within primary CEP:** Can the acquisition modes that worked for early signal (organic, earned, paid) scale at a viable cost?\n- **Audience acquisition cost (CAC) trajectory:** What does it cost to acquire one engaged viewer, and is that cost decreasing, flat, or increasing?\n- **Audience retention:** Do acquired viewers stay? What is the 30-day/60-day retention rate? Does retention improve as content library grows? This is critical — acquisition without retention is a leaky bucket.\n- **Flywheel mechanics:** Does the content generate compounding growth? Do earlier episodes get discovered by new audience members who find later content?\n- **Community sustainability:** Do community signals from Stages 2–4 scale? Does community persist between episodes? Does it generate its own content?\n- **Adjacent CEP expansion:** Can content that works in the primary CEP also win in adjacent entry points at acceptable cost?\n- **Unit economics:** What is the investment efficiency: for every €1 invested in growth, what return in engagement, reach, and retention?\nBlock 3 is tested in Stage 5. It is where the team proves growth is repeatable, not one-off.\n**Unit economics in this system** = audience acquisition cost + retention cost only. Not broader business metrics or monetization. The core question: what does it cost to acquire and keep one engaged audience member, and does that math work?\n---\n### How the Blocks relate\n**Strict sequence: B1 → B2 → B3.**\nIf B1 is denied, everything else is irrelevant. If B2 is denied, B3 is premature. If B3 is denied, there is no business.\n**Forward cascade:** Confirming B1 raises confidence in B2. Confirming B2 raises confidence in B3.\n**Feedback loop:** Results from later experiments update earlier hypotheses. A Stage 4 series with unexpected audience demographics is new evidence for the Target Audience hypothesis in B1. A Stage 3 artifact that generates unexpected traction in a different entry point is new evidence for the CEP Map. The team re-rates affected hypotheses at the next meeting, and updated scores may change priorities.\n---\n### Where classic product concepts get answered\n<table header-row=\"true\">\n<tr>\n<td>Concept</td>\n<td>Where it lives</td>\n<td>How it's tested</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Competitive Landscape</td>\n<td>Block 1, Stage 1</td>\n<td>CEP Map: \"What does a person in this entry point currently do?\"</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Value Proposition</td>\n<td>Block 1 (hypothesized), Stages 2–3 (validated)</td>\n<td>\"For a person in \\[CEP\\], our content does \\[X\\] that alternatives cannot\"</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Competitive Advantage</td>\n<td>Block 2, discovered by end of Stage 3</td>\n<td>Grounded in data from experiments, not declared upfront</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Product Solution (Story Direction)</td>\n<td>Block 2, Stages 1–4</td>\n<td>Refined through experiments within primary CEP; format, length, platform, tone are CEP-driven</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>MVP</td>\n<td>Stage 2</td>\n<td>First artifacts ARE the minimum viable product. Category Entry Points: Practical Guidance\nA Category Entry Point is a situation or context in which a person experiences a need that leads them to seek content in our category. It is a moment — not a demographic.\nFor horror/thriller:\n- \"Friday night alone, want adrenaline\" — lean-back, long-form, immersive\n- \"Commute, 15 minutes, need distraction\" — short-form, mobile, high-hook\n- \"With friends, want shared fear experience\" — social, shareable, reaction-worthy\nEach entry point implies different format, length, platform, and tone. The same JTBD activates differently in different contexts.\n### CEP vs. JTBD vs. Target Audience\n- **JTBD:** What does a person need? \"I want to be captivated by something thrilling\"\n- **CEP:** When and where does that need become active? \"Friday night alone, scrolling for something to commit to\"\n- **Target Audience:** Who, specifically, in this moment? \"A 26-year-old who finished work exhausted and wants to feel alive again before bed\"\nJTBD without CEP is too abstract to test. CEP without JTBD is shallow. All three together form a complete, testable unit.\n### CEP as strategic variable\nCEPs are not just context for experiments. They are the competitive lens through which we evaluate content strength. The same story direction can be weak in one CEP and dominant in another. Different CEPs have different competitors, different positioning, different advantages.\n- Friday night alone: Netflix horror (long), comfort rewatches (familiar), YouTube compilations (ad-supported)\n- Commute 15 min: TikTok (frictionless), podcasts (audio), Instagram Reels (social)\nThe team's task is not to find the \"best\" CEP — it is to find the CEPs where we have the strongest competitive position. Where is the current alternative weakest? Where is there room to enter? Where do our Story Direction's specific strengths match the gap? This is discovered through experiments, not assumed beforehand.\n---\n## 5. Hypothesis Format and Scoring\n### Standard hypothesis format\nEvery hypothesis across all Blocks is written in one format:\n> **We believe** \\[subject: the Target Audience\\] **in** \\[CEP context: the entry point situation\\] **will** \\[behaviour: respond to, seek out, return to, share\\] \\[the direction or element being tested\\]; **falsified if** \\[measurable condition: a specific observable outcome that would disprove the hypothesis\\].\nThe falsification condition must be pre-registered — written before the experiment runs. A hypothesis without a falsification condition is not testable; it is an assertion.\n### Hypothesis examples from DRAGN cascade\n**Stage 1 / Block 1 — Audience & CEP Hypothesis:**\n> We believe people 20–30 have an unmet need for high-adrenaline content that combines sci-fi tension with survival horror — something they can experience together and talk about after; falsified if qualitative interviews reveal current alternatives (Netflix horror, video games, escape rooms) adequately serve this need.\n**Stage 2 / Block 2 — Hook & Promise Hypothesis:**\n> We believe a 60-second teaser framed as \"leaked corporate security footage of a drone malfunction during a team retreat\" will stop scrolling among people in the primary CEP (Friday night social viewing, adrenaline seekers) and create genre recognition (tech horror + survival); falsified if organic engagement rate falls below 4% with fewer than 10% of comments showing AI/drone/survival recognition.\n**Stage 3 / Block 2 — Component Hypothesis:**\n> We believe the Conflict component (conspiracy reaching protagonist's family) will generate independent audience investment among people in the primary CEP (solo evening viewing); falsified if isolated Conflict test produces fewer than 50% of Stage 2 engagement rate with no theory or speculation comments.\n**Stage 5 / Block 3 — Growth & Scaling Hypothesis:**\n> We believe TikTok acquisition in the primary CEP can scale to 50K+ viewers in 2 months while maintaining CAC below €0.12; falsified if CAC exceeds €0.15 or if click-through-to-play falls below 8%.\n### Scoring: Confidence and Opportunity\nEvery hypothesis is rated by each committee member independently, before group discussion.\n**Confidence (1–10):** How much evidence supports this hypothesis?\n<table header-row=\"true\">\n<tr>\n<td>Score</td>\n<td>Meaning</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>1–2</td>\n<td>Gut feeling, no data. \"I just think so.\"</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>3–4</td>\n<td>Anecdotal: a few conversations, pattern noticed in passing.</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>5–6</td>\n<td>Moderate evidence: several data points, some research, consistent signals.</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>7–8</td>\n<td>Strong evidence: systematic research, multiple confirming sources.</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>9–10</td>\n<td>Near-certain: extensive validation, hard data, proven in practice.</td>\n</tr>\n</table>\n**Opportunity (1–10):** If this hypothesis is confirmed, how much does it open up the route to success?\n<table header-row=\"true\">\n<tr>\n<td>Score</td>\n<td>Meaning</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>1–2</td>\n<td>Confirming it changes almost nothing. A nice-to-know.</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>3–4</td>\n<td>Confirms a useful piece of the puzzle, but doesn't reshape the picture.</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>5–6</td>\n<td>Opens a meaningful stretch of the route. Multiple downstream hypotheses benefit.</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>7–8</td>\n<td>Confirmation would significantly accelerate progress. Reshapes the map.</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>9–10</td>\n<td>If true, this changes everything. The entire direction pivots on this.</td>\n</tr>\n</table>\n**Effort (1–10):** Tiebreaker only. How expensive is it to test this? 1 = a conversation, 10 = a full production.\n**Prospect Score** = Avg Confidence × Avg Opportunity (0–100 scale). Ranks hypotheses by strategic priority.\n### Prioritization: The Rule of Three\nOnce scored, the list is sorted by Prospect Score and cut to the top three. These are the only active hypotheses at this layer until one is confirmed, denied, or the team re-scores.\nThree is the working number. Three hypotheses can be held in mind simultaneously, discussed without losing track, and presented to a room without confusion. More than three and the system drifts — the team debates everything and tests nothing.\n### Rating divergence as a signal\nWhen committee members' ratings diverge significantly — one person gives Opportunity 9, another gives 3 — this is not a problem to average away. It is a strategic signal. The team is operating on different mental models.\nLarge divergence on a high-Opportunity hypothesis may signal that the team needs to align on fundamentals before testing anything.\n### For a full worked example, see Appendix A: DRAGN Cascade.\n---\n## 6. The Stages\nThe stages are the operational sequence. Each stage has a defined scope, a cost ceiling, and a gate decision. No stage should spend at the level of the next stage.\n### Growth as Means vs. Growth as Object\nBefore describing stages, one distinction is critical.\n**Stages 2–4: Growth as a Means.** The team tries channels to find 50–100 people in the primary CEP who can see the artifacts. The goal is eyeballs for the product test. If TikTok doesn't work, try YouTube Shorts. Growth is a tool to enable product validation. Data gathered — which channels worked, which audience segments responded, what organic retention looked like — becomes input for Stage 5 design.\n**Stage 5: Growth as the Object.** Product is proven. Now the team systematically tests whether a scalable acquisition engine exists. Can we acquire thousands in the primary CEP cost-effectively? Do they retain? Can we expand to adjacent CEPs? Growth itself is the thing being validated.\nThis mirrors lean startup logic: value hypothesis (does the product create value?) vs. growth hypothesis (can we build a scalable engine to reach the market?). Stages 2–4 answer value. Stage 5 answers growth.\n---\n### Prep Stage — Triage\n<table header-row=\"true\">\n<colgroup>\n<col>\n<col width=\"448\">\n</colgroup>\n<tr>\n<td>Field</td>\n<td>Content</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Question being tested**</td>\n<td>Initial plausibility of Block 1 (Is there an audience in identifiable entry points?)</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**What happens**</td>\n<td>The author presents initial hypotheses: <br>• audience need (JTBD hypothesis), <br>• situations where it activates (initial CEP thinking), <br>• and why this Story Direction serves it (Direction Thesis). <br><br>Team selects one direction, registers initial hypotheses, and confirms rights/viability.</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Entry paths**</td>\n<td>Audience Insight (standard) or Existing Script (Stage 4 entry with prior approval)</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Key outputs**</td>\n<td>• Chosen direction + rationale <br>• Provisional Direction Thesis <br>• Hypothesis Registry entry <br>• Measurement Plan <br>• Rights/viability note • Initial CEP thinking</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Gate question**</td>\n<td>Is this direction plausible enough to research?</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Decision**</td>\n<td>• Go → Stage 1 <br>• Hold → rights/viability unclear <br>• Kill → no credible promise</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Timing**</td>\n<td>2–4 days</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Budget**</td>\n<td>€0–150 (low: €0, mid: €50, high: €150)</td>\n</tr>\n</table>\n---\n### Stage 1 — Audience & Entry Point Research\n<table header-row=\"true\">\n<colgroup>\n<col>\n<col width=\"449\">\n</colgroup>\n<tr>\n<td>Field</td>\n<td>Content</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Question being tested**</td>\n<td>Block 1 (Is there an underserved audience in identifiable entry points?)</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**What happens**</td>\n<td>Dedicated research sprint validating Block 1 hypotheses in cascade order. <br><br>1. Author attends and reports. Full IC attendance not required at weekly meetings — IC convenes for Gate Reviews only (biweekly, Stages 2–5).\n#### Part 1: What did we learn? (10 min)\n- Author and team report experiment results from past week\n- Raw data: quotes, numbers, observations — no interpretation yet\n- Navigation Lead confirms which hypotheses were tested and whether falsification conditions were met\n#### Part 2: Re-rate (5 min, silent)\n- Each member updates Confidence and Opportunity scores for affected hypotheses\n- Done silently to prevent anchoring\n#### Part 3: Discuss and cascade (15 min)\n- Review updated scores — where did they change?\n- Trace cascade: what does this result mean for connected hypotheses? For other Blocks?\n- Surface new hypotheses: did this experiment reveal something unexpected?\n- CEP check: did the experiment produce signal from outside primary CEP? Log as \"discovered, untested\" for Stage 5.\n- Cross-direction check: does this learning affect other Story Directions in the Slate?\n#### Part 4: Next step (5 min)\n- Look at updated Prospect Score ranking\n- Top hypothesis is next to test\n- Design cheapest experiment that could meaningfully move Confidence\n- Assign: who runs this, by when?\n- Navigation Lead confirms: is a Gate Review due?\n---\n### What happens when a hypothesis is denied\nClassify the failure first:\n<table header-row=\"true\">\n<tr>\n<td>Type</td>\n<td>What happened</td>\n<td>Response</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Content failure**</td>\n<td>The hypothesis was wrong — the need, CEP, or claim doesn't hold</td>\n<td>Re-rate all connected hypotheses; cascade updates</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Form failure**</td>\n<td>The hypothesis might be right, but experiment couldn't test it properly</td>\n<td>Redesign the experiment</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Timing failure**</td>\n<td>The hypothesis might be right, but cultural moment is wrong</td>\n<td>Flag for later</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**Execution failure**</td>\n<td>The experiment was badly run</td>\n<td>Re-run it properly</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>**CEP mismatch**</td>\n<td>The content works, but not in target entry point</td>\n<td>No cascade — log as discovery. Revisit in Stage 5.</td>\n</tr>\n</table>\nOnly content failures trigger cascade updates. CEP mismatch is a special case — it's a discovery, not a failure. The content may serve a different audience moment. Valuable data, but doesn't invalidate the current hypothesis about the primary CEP.\nWhen content failure cascades, new hypotheses often emerge. Denying \"solo horror fans\" might surface \"true crime enthusiasts\" as alternative. The cascade is generative, not just reductive.\n---\n### What data gets enriched over time\nThe system starts with purely subjective scores — and that is enough. As the team runs experiments, scores get enriched with real data:\n- Audience conversations → Confidence in Target Audience and CEP hypotheses becomes research-informed\n- Concept tests → Direction hypothesis gets real signal\n- Engagement metrics → Commitment hypotheses get quantified\n- Channel and acquisition data → experiment economics become cost-informed; feed budget model\n- Growth and community data (Stage 4+) → Confidence in Block 3 hypotheses: CAC trajectory, flywheel signals, community sustainability\n- CEP performance data → CEP Map evolves from research artifact into data-backed strategic asset\nThe system never requires quantitative data to function. It always welcomes it. Each piece of evidence makes the subjective scores stronger, more calibrated, more trustworthy.\n---\n## 8. Technical Implementation\nThis system is built in Notion. The technical infrastructure consists of linked databases supporting the scoring and cascade process.\n### Database structure\n**Hypothesis MDB** — the central database. Each hypothesis is a record with: name and description, Block (B1 / B2 / B3), sub-type (JTBD / CEP / Target Audience / Problems / Competition / Direction / Channel / Hook+Promise / Component / Series / Scalable Acquisition / CAC / Retention / Flywheel / Community Sustainability / Adjacent CEP / Rights / Budget / Route to Market), status (Planning → In progress → Validated / Invalidated / Archived), current stage, **target CEP**, linked ratings, linked experiments, computed Prospect Score.\n**Hypothesis Ratings MDB** — individual committee ratings. Each rating links to a hypothesis and records: Confidence (1–10), Opportunity (1–10), Effort (1–10, tiebreaker), rater identity. Ratings collected independently before discussion.\n**Experiments MDB** — experiment log linked to hypotheses. Records: hypothesis tested, **target CEP**, experiment design and cost, result (Confirmed / Denied / Inconclusive), failure type (if denied): Content / Form / Timing / Execution / **CEP Mismatch**, cascade notes.\n**CEP Map MDB** — new database. Each entry point is a record with: situation description, frequency estimate, current alternatives, gap assessment, status (Primary / Adjacent / Discovered / Untested), linked hypotheses, linked experiments, performance data (populated from Stage 2 onward).\n### Computed fields\n**Prospect Score** = Avg Confidence × Avg Opportunity (0–100 scale).\n**Action Priority** = derived from Prospect Score and Effort. When two hypotheses have similar Prospect Scores, the cheaper-to-test hypothesis gets higher Action Priority.\n### Views\nThe primary view groups hypotheses by Block (B1 → B2 → B3), sorted by Prospect Score descending. This gives the committee a ready-to-use priority list at every meeting.\nA secondary view groups by Stage, showing which hypotheses belong to the current stage and which await future stages.\nA **CEP view** groups hypotheses by entry point, showing all hypotheses related to each CEP and their current status. One experiment, many updates. Type 1 inform Type 2, Type 2 inform Type 3. Experiments change probabilities, which change priorities. The map redraws with each reading.\n\t**Block:** A cluster of related sub-questions answered through coordinated experiments. B1 (Audience): JTBD, CEPs, Target Audience, Audience Problems, Competitive Landscape. B2 (Product): Hook, Components, Series sustain, Early reach. B3 (Growth Engine): Scalable acquisition, retention, flywheel, adjacent expansion. Answered in strict sequence: B1 → B2 → B3.\n\t**Unit Economics (in this system):** Audience acquisition cost and retention cost specifically. Not broader business metrics or monetization. What does it cost to acquire and keep one engaged audience member, and does that math work?\n\t**Category Entry Point (CEP):** A situation or context in which a person experiences a need that leads them to seek content in our category. Not a demographic — a moment. Example: \"Friday night alone, want adrenaline.\"\n\t**Primary CEP:** The entry point designated in Stage 1 as focus for product validation (Stages 2–4). Subject to cascade — if experiments show it's not working, the team re-evaluates by end of Stage 3. Commitment Phase (Stages 4–5) operates with a committed CEP.\n\t**CEP Map:** Prioritized list of entry points with situation description, frequency, current alternatives, gap assessment, and status. Created in Stage 1, updated through pipeline. Key artifact of the unified system.\n\t**CEP Mismatch:** Failure classification where content works, but not in target entry point. Neither content nor form failure — it's a discovery. Logged, not cascaded, revisited in Stage 5.\n\t**JTBD (Job to be Done):** The root hypothesis of Block 1. An audience need that current content fails to serve. Not a genre preference — a functional or emotional need. The foundation the entire cascade stands on.\n\t**Target Audience:** A specific person in specific situations who feels the JTBD most acutely in primary CEP. Described in a way that allows the team to find them and recruit 50–100 for experiments. Not a demographic — a circumstance and a findability criterion.\n\t**Direction Thesis:** The author's claim that a specific combination of World, Character, Conflict, and Promise can serve the JTBD better than alternatives in primary CEP. The foundational hypothesis of Block 2.\n\t**Channel hypothesis:** The team's claim about which platform and format gives best access to Target Audience in primary CEP. Part of Block 2. CEP-driven: the entry point constrains the format.\n\t**Growth as Means (Stages 2–4):** Using acquisition channels to find 50–100 people for product testing. Goal is eyeballs, not optimization.\n\t**Growth as Object (Stage 5):** Systematically validating that a scalable, repeatable acquisition engine exists. Growth itself is the thing being validated.\n\t**Value Hypothesis vs. Growth Hypothesis:** Stages 2–4 answer \"does the product create value?\" Stage 5 answers \"can we build a scalable engine to reach the market?\"\n\t**Product-Market Fit:** Gate 4 — 50–100 early adopters commit within primary CEP. PMF is CEP-specific.\n\t**Experiment economics:** The unit cost of learning — what it costs to run a single experiment from artifact creation to audience delivery to data collection. Tracked from Stage 2; feeds into budget model.\n\t**Flywheel:** A growth dynamic where each piece of content brings in new audience members who then discover older content, increasing total engagement without proportional investment. Key evidence for Block 3.\n\t**CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost):** Cost to acquire one engaged viewer. Tracked per acquisition mode, per CEP, per stage. The trend matters more than the absolute number.\n\t**Retention:** Percentage of acquired viewers who return after 30 days or 60 days. Critical to Block 3 validation alongside acquisition. Proves engagement, not just novelty-driven trial.\n\t**Standard hypothesis format:** \"We believe \\[subject\\] in \\[CEP context\\] will \\[behaviour\\]; falsified if \\[measurable condition\\].\" Pre-registered falsification condition required before any experiment.\n\t**Measurement Plan:** Document locking each hypothesis's metric, threshold, and read window before any artifact is produced. Owned by Navigation Lead.\n\t**Confidence (1–10):** How much evidence supports a hypothesis. 1 = gut feeling, 10 = validated with data.\n\t**Opportunity (1–10):** How much confirming a hypothesis opens up the route. 1 = nice-to-know, 10 = changes everything.\n\t**Prospect Score:** Confidence × Opportunity. Ranks hypotheses by strategic value — what to test next. Within a stage: drives prioritization. At a gate: Confidence component drives Go/Pivot/Kill recommendation.\n\t**Rule of Three:** At any moment, maximum three active hypotheses per sub-type. Enough for optionality, few enough for focus.\n\t**Signal Phase (Stages 1–3):** The phase where team is choosing between competing possibilities. Primary decision tool: Prospect Score ranking.\n\t**Commitment Phase (Stages 4–5):** The phase where team has committed to one direction. Primary decision tool: failure-mode analysis.\n\t***P(success)*:** The team's collective probability estimate (0–100%) that a direction reaches commercial viability. Updated weekly. A compass, not a formula. Rising = gaining sea room. Flat after 3 experiments = structural problem. Falling = direction weakening.\n\t**Gate Memo:** Formal Go/Pivot/Kill recommendation from Navigation Lead (Stages 1–4) or Growth Lead + Navigation Lead (Stage 5). Summarizes hypothesis tested, Confidence movement, *P(success)* movement, and recommendation. No Gate Memo = no slot in Gate Review.\n\t**Content failure:** A denied hypothesis where the underlying claim was wrong. The only failure type that triggers cascade updates.\n\t**Packaging Sprint (post-Gate 5):** Execution phase where team assembles IP Dossier, growth data package, and investment case. Not validation — presentation.\n\t**Rights/viability note:** Mandatory gate-blocking document at Prep Stage. If rights are unresolved, direction is held — not killed — until clarified. Re-checked as Gate 5 precondition.\n\t**Cross-direction cascade:** Weekly check — does a learning from one Story Direction affect others in the Slate?\n\t**Feedback Loop:** The principle that cascade flows forward (B1→B2→B3) but also backward to earlier blocks. Results from later experiments update earlier hypotheses. The map is always dynamic, not linear.\n</content>\n</page>"}
